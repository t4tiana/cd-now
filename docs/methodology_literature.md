## A standard trend benchmark

Hyndman and Athanasopoulos, _Forecasting: Principles and Practice_, 3rd ed. (OTexts, 2021 online edition), [§7.7, “Nonlinear regression”](https://otexts.com/fpp3/nonlinear-regression.html), gives the log-linear trend form `log(y_t) = β₀ + β₁t + ε_t` in the “Forecasting with a nonlinear trend” passage. I fitted this form to fully observed cohort age-revenue rates as a [benchmark](../reports/trend_benchmark.md) against the provisional three-window extension. The source supports the statistical form, not CDnow's specific trend, my choice of starting age window, or long-horizon accuracy.

## Why I used the three-window forecast provisionally

Our current rule groups 30-day customer-age revenue rates into three-window blocks, compares the latest with the previous block, and extends the resulting geometric decline. **Three is an analyst-selected smoothing length, not a published norm.** A shorter block reacts more to recent changes but is noisier; a longer block smooths noise but may miss a genuine shift. That tradeoff is why [P3.2](../reports/forecast_extensions.md) compares 2, 3, 4, and 6 windows and tests them on later _fully observed_ age windows. The tests support using a declining rather than flat continuation over those short withheld spans; they are too short and overlapping to establish one winner for a future year.

**My reasoning:** I used a simple, transparent extension because the single cohort supplies only 15 fully comparable age windows. I withheld later observed windows to see how alternative smoothing lengths would have predicted them, and I show the resulting forecast range. Three windows is my provisional working choice, not an industry rule. I would want additional cohorts before treating it as a reliable long-horizon forecast

This is consistent with time-series cross-validation and short-series cautions above. I could not find academic literature endorsing three windows specifically.

An alternative is to fit a published customer-transaction model or a standard trend-smoothing model and compare its holdout performance with the current rule. That could reduce reliance on the arbitrary window length, but it introduces other assumptions and fitting choices, and the one-cohort observation window still cannot validate years of future behavior. It is a materially different forecast approach.

## How I use the 80% eligibility illustration

**No reviewed source gives a normal 80% eligibility fraction for this kind of proposed contract.** It was a round, one-at-a-time stress, not a base rate. Literature supports _examining sensitivity to uncertain inputs_; it cannot supply a number whose value is determined by the repayment definition, refunds, attribution, cash collection, and CDnow's records. The correct diligence request is a reconciliation from transaction-file dollars to contractually eligible, collected cohort revenue.

The decision-focused measure is a **break-even eligibility threshold**. For advance `A`, full repayment of `1.2A` requires `2.4A` of eligible revenue. If modeled reported dollars through a stated horizon are `R`, and a fraction `e` is eligible and collected on the modeled schedule, the cap can be reached by that horizon only if `e ≥ 2.4A / R`. For the illustrative $1 million advance, the threshold is **76.1%** in the provisional Q4 cohort forecast, **81.1%** under the early-repeat downside, and **82.3%** under the top-quarter-spending downside, through December 31, 2000. These are _model-implied thresholds_, not estimates that CDnow meets them. The repayment dates also depend on when eligible cash is collected and remitted.

**My reasoning:** Instead of assuming an eligibility rate, I show the minimum fraction of modeled dollars that must actually qualify and be collected for the cap to be reached, then ask CDnow to reconcile its sales ledger to that definition. The 80% row is only an illustration of sensitivity and does not drive my estimate of eligible revenue.
